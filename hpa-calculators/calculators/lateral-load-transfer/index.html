Lateral Load Transfer Distribution

In a corner load moves to the outside wheels. LLTD is the share of that taken by the front axle. The figure that decides balance.

How to use this

This is a tuning tool, not a prediction. It cannot tell you what your car will do. There are too many variables outside it, from tyre construction and pressures to dampers, aero and the corner itself. What it does well is tell you how big a change you just made, before you spend money or track time finding out. So it is worth most when used as a loop.

  1. Establish a baseline. Enter the car as it sits now and note the LLTD. Name it in the Setup box and copy the link. That is your reference point. Put the figure in the Baseline LLTD field and every later change is reported as a move from it.
  2. See which lever has authority. Only the elastic share responds to springs and bars. Geometric is set by roll centre heights, and unsprung is fixed once the car is built. If elastic dominates, a spring or bar change will move the balance meaningfully; if it does not, expect to be disappointed.
  3. Try one change and read the move. The sliders in the results panel move each rate in catalogue steps. Half a kgf/mm, five N/mm, twenty-five lb/in, so whatever you land on is a part you could order. The trial figure appears in amber alongside your setup, and your inputs are untouched until you decide to keep it.
  4. Test it, then come back. Whether the new number is better is a question only the track answers. If it is, you now have a validated figure and know what produced it. If the change fixed the balance but cost you something else. Ride, aero platform, kerb tolerance. Come back and reach the same LLTD a different way, for instance a stiffer spring paired with a softer bar.
  5. Build a set of setups. Save each one you have actually validated, with its LLTD. What counts as neutral is something you find by testing, and it moves with conditions — a car that is neutral at 55% front in the dry may want 53% in the wet. Those are two saved setups, or two bar positions, not two different cars.

Two things worth knowing about the figures. Lateral g does not change LLTD. The model is linear, so more cornering moves more load at both ends in the same proportion; the g figure is there for the wheel loads and the lift limit. And a higher front share means the front axle takes more of the transfer, which is a fact about the setup rather than a verdict on the handling.

Assumptions and limits
  • Valid only while all four wheels carry load. Past the lift figure shown, none of this applies.
  • The model is linear, so load transfer distribution does not change with lateral G. Real tyres and dampers are not linear.
  • Tyres are rigid. Their vertical stiffness is ignored, which makes the car slightly stiffer in roll than modelled.
  • Unsprung masses and their centres of gravity are per corner.
  • Motion ratios are wheel travel over component travel, taken as constant through the travel.
  • Bar rate is at the arm tip with the other side held. Springs contribute half their wheel rate times track squared to roll stiffness, a bar its full rate, because a bar resists the wheels moving oppositely.
  • Roll centres are taken at ride height and treated as fixed. They migrate as the suspension moves, so geometric transfer is only approximately constant.
  • Steady-state cornering only. No aero load, no transient weight transfer, no compliance in bushes or chassis.
