Nineteen filaments with their published mechanical figures and the print settings that go with them. Pick the ones you are choosing between and compare them directly.

How to use this

Choosing a filament is a set of trades, not a ranking. The strongest materials are the hardest to print, the toughest are the least stiff, and the cheapest does most jobs perfectly well.

  1. Start from the part, not the material. What will break it? A bracket that flexes wants stiffness. A clip that snaps wants ductility. Something near a turbo wants heat resistance. That decides which bar you read first.
  2. Shortlist across families. Comparing four nylons tells you less than comparing a nylon, a carbon filled grade and PETG, because the differences between families are far larger than within one.
  3. Check what it takes to print. The printability line is the one that decides whether a material is realistic on the machine you own. A material needing an enclosure and a 350 degree hotend is not a choice if you have neither.
  4. Read the bars as relative, the numbers as absolute. Bars are scaled against the strongest material in the guide, so they show where a material sits in the field. The figures below them are the published values. The radar adds ease of printing as a fifth axis, so a shape shows the whole trade at once.
  5. Treat the figures as typical, not as a datasheet. These are representative of each family. Two brands of PETG can differ by more than PETG differs from PLA, and printed parts are weaker than moulded test bars in the layer direction.

Nothing here accounts for layer adhesion, which is usually what actually fails on a printed part. A material that is strong across the layers can still split along them.

Assumptions and limits
  • Figures are typical published values for each family, not a particular brand. Spools vary, sometimes by more than the gap between two families.
  • They come from moulded test specimens. A printed part is weaker, and much weaker across the layers, which is where most printed parts actually fail.
  • Strength and stiffness bars average the tensile and bending figures, since a printed part is usually loaded both ways.
  • Bars are scaled against the highest value in the guide, not against your selection, so a bar means the same thing whichever materials are on screen.
  • Ductility is scaled logarithmically. Elongation runs from 2% to 400%, and on a linear scale every rigid material would sit at zero.
  • Heat resistance is rated on glass transition, the point at which a printed part starts losing stiffness. Deflection temperature is in the table too, but datasheets quote it at two different loads, so it is not comparable across the whole set.
  • Impact strength is missing for the nylons and the high performance materials, and overview text for the carbon filled and high performance sets, because the source had no figures.
  • PEI, PEEK and PEKK figures were corrected against published datasheets. PEI is amorphous and has no melting point.
  • Printability is derived from the drying, enclosure and temperature requirements. It is a guide to what the machine needs, not a measure of how well the material prints.
  • On the radar it appears inverted, as ease, so that outward means better on every axis. It is scaled against the most demanding material in the guide.
  • Nothing here covers cost, availability, UV stability, chemical compatibility or food safety.
