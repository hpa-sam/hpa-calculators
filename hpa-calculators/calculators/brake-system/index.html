Brake System Calculator

What share of the braking the front axle should take, the line pressures that need, and where it leaves the pedal and the bias bar.

How to use this

Braking wants each axle to work in proportion to the load it is carrying. Load moves forward as the car slows, so the share the front should take climbs with deceleration, and a fixed bias bar cannot follow it. Every setting is therefore a compromise, and the useful question is which deceleration to be right at.

  1. Enter the brakes you have. Calipers and rotors are the fixed part of the problem; the cylinders are what you choose around them, so the calculator recommends a pair and lists the alternatives beside it.
  2. Pick the deceleration that matters. Not the highest you can reach. The one where getting the balance wrong costs you, usually hard braking into your slowest corner. That is your design point.
  3. Read the required bias, then the sweep. The table shows what the car wants at every deceleration. The spread across it is the compromise you are making; a car with a low centre of gravity has a flatter spread and is easier to set up.
  4. Judge a pair on its adjustment window, not just on whether it works. A bar parked near one end of its travel has nothing left in the direction you will eventually want it. The pairs are ranked on bar centring first for that reason. A slightly heavier pedal with the bar at 55% beats a perfect pedal with the bar at 64%.
  5. Read the plot as one question: is the thick blue line in the green? The black line is optimal bias. Green is the target. Front of optimal, but by no more than 7 points. Red is below optimal, where the rear locks first, and that is the region to stay out of at any cost. Grey above is safe but the front is doing more work than it needs to. The thin blue lines are what the bias bar can reach at each end of its travel.
  6. Use a valve to bend the line, not to trim it. Without one the blue line is flat, so it can only be in the green over a narrow range. A proportioning valve kinks it upward so it follows the optimal line, which widens that range. Watch the band figure as you change the knee point and slope.
  7. Then judge the pedal. Effort and travel are what you actually feel. Around 30 to 40mm of travel with an effort you can modulate repeatedly is the target; a pedal that is too light is as hard to be precise with as one that is too heavy.
  8. Validate on track and come back. Lock-up tells you which end is over-braked far more reliably than any calculation. Adjust, note what worked, and re-enter it so the next change starts from something real.

Pad friction is the least trustworthy input here: a figure from a catalogue is one temperature on one rotor, and it moves with heat, bedding and speed. Treat the pressures as a design starting point rather than a specification.

Assumptions and limits
  • Steady-state deceleration with all four wheels on the ground. Past the lift point shown, none of these figures apply.
  • Pad friction is a single constant. It moves with temperature, speed and bedding, which is the largest source of error here.
  • Both axles are assumed to run the same tyre compound, and tyre load sensitivity is ignored. That shifts the ideal bias slightly rearward.
  • Tyres are rigid and the loaded radius from the tyre size is used throughout.
  • Pad depth is the radial depth of the pad. Effective radius is the mean of the swept annulus.
  • A proportioning valve divides the rear pressure rise above its knee point. It can hold rear pressure back, never add to it.
  • Downforce is held constant at the braking speed, though in reality it falls away as the car slows.
  • Thermal limits, hose expansion, pad taper and caliper flex are not modelled.
  • Pedal effort multiplied by pedal travel is fixed by the torque required, the pad friction and the rotor radius. Cylinder bore and pedal ratio move you along that curve but cannot shift it.
  • Figures are a design starting point. Lock-up on track tells you which end is over-braked far more reliably.
